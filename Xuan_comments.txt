Regarding the research questions, since I helped out with the ThoughtTracing paper and know a bit about what the lead author tried, I thought it'd be helpful if "pre-register" some of my takes about what you're likely to find:
How do these approaches perform on newer, open weight models? And on newer ToM benchmarks? Unclear, will be interesting to find out! Just be prepared for not-so-great results if you use smaller models (given that we had to use 70B+ models to achieve strong results).
What if ThoughtTracing's belief hypotheses were not free text, but instead structured tuples? For instance, like in AutoToM, in an explicit (observation, belief, goal) tuple? Hyunwoo tried this initially, and it worked less well than free text.
Could iteratively summarizing the trajectory in ThoughtTracing help with performance? Can't remember if we tried this, but IIRC a fair amount of context was necessary for the final answer-generating LLM call to do well.
Could we improve performance by modifying how new hypotheses are generated in the self-correction step of ThoughtTracing? IIRC Hyunwoo tried a lot of variants of this and the reported version is what worked.
Would crafting more complex master prompts for ThoughtTracing help with performance? Maybe!
How much of AutoToM's performance improvement can be attributed to increased test-time compute? We did sorta test this for ThoughtTracing by comparing against o1 and o3, you could try comparing AutoToM against a raw reasoning model for comparison (not sure if they already did this).
How can we combine AutoToM and ThoughtTracing's architectures? Currently, each ThoughtTracing particle is an LLM-generated belief hypothesis h_t^(i) together with its probability weight w_t^(i). What if we structure each particle as a causal Bayesian network tuple of latent variables h_t^(i) = (o_t^(i), b_t^(i), g_t^(i)) (as in AutoToM)? See above comment on Hyunwoo trying this and finding that it worked less well -- it's not clear to me yet when and why structured hypotheses work better than free text.
Can we use actual log-probabilities provided by open-weight models to improve performance, while bypassing the qualitative multiple-choice approximations required by closed-source APIs? This might require certain calibration techniques that take into account effects of length, word choice, etc. on the actual log-likelihoods. I can't remember if we directly tried this (we may have), but I think this will almost certainly work less than verbalized probabilities -- logprobs are much more sensitive to the length of text than verbalized probs. Note that AutoToM actually uses logprobs for the action likelihoods in an interesting way -- first use a prompt that asks for a verbalized qualitative probability (Likely, vs. Unlikely), then query the logprobs of generating "Likely". This is different than the ThoughtTracing approach, and it'd be interesting to compare the two approaches.
